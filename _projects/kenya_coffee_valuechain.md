Kenyan coffee passes through several hands between the farm and the roaster. Each step is licensed and regulated under the Coffee Act, 2026 (see [Kenya coffee laws](/projects/kenya-coffee-laws.html) and [coffee licences](/projects/kenya-coffee-licensing.html)). This is the typical route for washed Arabica, which makes up most of Kenya's crop.

## 1. Growing

Coffee is grown in the highlands, mostly between 1,400 and 2,000 metres, around Mount Kenya, the Aberdares, Nakuru, Machakos, Kisii and Mount Elgon. The main varieties are SL28 and SL34, alongside the disease-resistant Ruiru 11 and Batian. Seed and seedlings come from certified sources, and nurseries are certified by the Coffee Research and Training Institute.

Most of Kenya's coffee comes from **smallholders**, who are members of [cooperative societies](/directory/cooperatives.html). The rest comes from **[estates](/directory/estates.html)**: farms of at least five acres, or producing at least 20,000 kg of cherry a year. Every grower is registered with its county.

## 2. Picking and delivering cherry

Ripe red cherry is hand-picked, mainly from October to December for the main crop, with a smaller "fly crop" around April to June. Smallholders deliver cherry to their cooperative's **[factory](/directory/factories.html)** (pulping station), where it is weighed on calibrated digital scales and recorded for each farmer.

## 3. Primary processing: cherry to parchment

At the factory, cherry is sorted, pulped to remove the skin and fruit, fermented, washed and soaked, then sun-dried on raised beds until it reaches the standard moisture content. The result is **parchment**: the bean still in its papery husk. Coffee dried whole, without pulping, is called **buni** (mbuni).

## 4. Milling and grading

The cooperative or estate competitively procures a licensed commercial miller, or mills its own coffee as a grower miller. At the dry mill, parchment is hulled and polished, then sorted by size and density into the Kenyan **[grades](/projects/kenya-coffee-grades.html)**: E, AA, AB, PB, C, TT, T and others. Each delivery gets an **out-turn number** for traceability. Samples go to liquorers for **cup-quality** assessment, and the miller sends a sample of each grade to the Coffee Board.

## 5. Warehousing

Clean coffee is stored in a county-licensed warehouse. The warehouseman issues a **coffee warrant** or **warehouse receipt** that records ownership, quantity and quality, and can be used as collateral.

## 6. Selling: auction or direct sale

- **The auction.** Most coffee is sold at the weekly auction of the **Nairobi Coffee Exchange**. The grower or its CMA-licensed broker catalogues the lots and sets a reserve price with the grower. Licensed [buyers](/directory/coffee-exporters.html) taste samples and bid in US dollars. See [auction prices](/transactions.html) and the [top buyers](/directory/coffee-buyers.html).
- **Direct sales.** Growers can sell directly to an overseas buyer or a local roaster. A sample goes to the exchange for quality and price discovery, the price must compare favourably with exchange prices, and the contract is reported to the Coffee Board.

## 7. Payment

Buyers pay by the **prompt date** (within five working days of the sale) into the **central settlement system**. Under the Coffee Act, 2026, proceeds must reach the grower (the cooperative or estate) within 14 days, and service providers' charges are settled directly. A cooperative may keep at most 10% of gross coffee earnings to cover its costs. Cooperatives then pay their members for the cherry they delivered.

## 8. Export

Buyers export green coffee to roasters worldwide, with a Coffee Board movement permit and certificates of origin and quality, and a KEPHIS phytosanitary certificate. A growing share is roasted in Kenya by county-licensed roasters for the local market.
