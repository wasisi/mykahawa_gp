Kenyan coffee passes through several hands between the farm and the roaster. Each step is licensed and regulated (see [Kenya coffee laws](/projects/kenya-coffee-laws.html) and [coffee licences](/projects/kenya-coffee-licensing.html)). This is the typical route for washed Arabica, which makes up most of Kenya's crop.

## 1. Growing

Coffee is grown in the highlands, mostly between 1,400 and 2,000 metres, around Mount Kenya, the Aberdares, Nakuru, Machakos, Kisii and Mount Elgon. The main varieties are SL28 and SL34, alongside the disease-resistant Ruiru 11 and Batian. Seed and seedlings must be certified (through KALRO's Coffee Research Institute) and raised in county-licensed nurseries.

Most of Kenya's coffee comes from **smallholders**, who are members of [cooperative societies](/directory/cooperatives.html). The rest comes from **[estates](/directory/estates.html)**: farms of at least five acres, or producing at least 20,000 kg of cherry a year. Every grower is registered with its county.

## 2. Picking and delivering cherry

Ripe red cherry is hand-picked, mainly from October to December for the main crop, with a smaller "fly crop" around April to June. Smallholders deliver cherry to their cooperative's **[factory](/directory/factories.html)** (pulping station), where it is weighed on calibrated digital scales and recorded for each farmer.

## 3. Primary processing: cherry to parchment

At the factory, cherry is sorted, pulped to remove the skin and fruit, fermented, washed and soaked, then sun-dried on raised beds until it reaches the standard moisture content. The result is **parchment**: the bean still in its papery husk. Coffee dried whole, without pulping, is called **buni** (mbuni).

## 4. Milling and grading

The cooperative or estate procures a licensed miller (a **[miller-marketer](/projects/kenya-coffee-glossary.html#miller-marketer)**), or mills its own coffee as a grower miller. At the dry mill, parchment is hulled and polished, then sorted by size and density into the Kenyan **[grades](/projects/kenya-coffee-grades.html)**: E, AA, AB, PB, C, TT, T and others. Each delivery gets an **out-turn number** for traceability. Milling charges are capped at KSh 4,000 per tonne, and samples go to liquorers for **cup-quality** assessment.

## 5. Warehousing

Clean coffee is stored in a county-licensed warehouse. The warehouseman issues a **coffee warrant** or **warehouse receipt** that records ownership, quantity and quality, and can be used as collateral.

## 6. Selling: auction or direct sale

- **The auction.** Most coffee is sold at the weekly auction of the **Nairobi Coffee Exchange**. The miller-marketer, grower marketer or a CMA-licensed broker catalogues the lots and sets a reserve price with the grower. Licensed [buyers](/directory/coffee-exporters.html) taste samples and bid in US dollars. See [auction prices](/transactions.html) and the [top buyers](/directory/coffee-buyers.html).
- **Direct sales.** Growers can sell directly to an overseas buyer or a local roaster. The contract is lodged with AFA, the coffee is inspected, and the price must compare favourably with the auction, where the direct-sale price acts as the reserve.

## 7. Payment

Buyers pay by the **prompt date** (within five working days of the sale) into the **direct settlement system**. Its banks pay the grower, and settle the miller, warehouseman and other service providers, within five working days. Cooperatives then pay their members for the cherry they delivered.

## 8. Export

Buyers export green coffee to roasters worldwide, with an AFA movement permit, a certificate of origin and quality, and a phytosanitary certificate. A growing share is roasted in Kenya by county-licensed roasters for the local market.
